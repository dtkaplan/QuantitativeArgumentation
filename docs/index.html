Preface

Notes to Pre-publication Reviewers

In 2023, I was challenged to develop two new core quantitative courses. This was part of a broader project to rethink the liberal arts curriculum from the ground up, and “core” meant something more stringent than usual. The courses would serve a requirement for all entering students to take exactly those courses. The courses filled a slot that, at almost all universities and colleges, is occupied by introductory statistics and pre-calculus/intro-calculus.

Students come from high school with widely varying expectations about where they will be placed in the mathematics curriculum, anywhere from Calc III to no place at all. This pair of courses was to be the common placement for all students, regardless of background.

The courses were to be closely aligned with a mission to foster critical thinking generally, as well as leadership in both the public and entrepreneurial realms. They were not to be a mere sample of college-level topics but a coherent introduction to methods genuinely useful in policy debates, commerce, and science.

This book is the text for one of the two courses. The paired course covers data science and statistics, including methods common to contemporary science as well as causal inference and the Bayesian framework.

A free, online version of the book will be published alongside this print edition. Exercises, discussion materials, and supplementary resources will also be available online.

Preface (for the to-be released version)

You, the reader, likely fall into one of three groups: students in college, students bound for college, or college instructors. A goal often claimed by colleges is that, among other things, they foster the development of critical thinking skills. While often used merely as a marketing slogan or mission statement, many college faculty press for the explicit teaching of critical thinking to be taught explicitly. This book is allied is allied with that goal.

This book has a particular emphasis on critical thinking with quantitative information. But there are broad themes in the explicit critical thinking movement that are worth keeping in mind when considering how to teach critical thinking. Among these are 1) finding, evaluating, and using information effectively, 2) translating data and knowledge into formats that highlight key relationships, 3) building sound arguments based on evidence and knowledge, and 4) being aware of your own thinking patterns, including the development of new patterns and finding the appropriate level of skepticism and to your own and other’s conclusions.

There is an argument to be made that traditional mathematics education serves these themes, but I think it is a weak argument. When it comes to (1), statistics is a much better fit than algebra. And while algebra does provide a format for knowledge as in (2), it is very limited, leaving no room for uncertainty or even data. The mathematical approach to building sound arguments is centered on proof and symbolic manipulation. But there are few settings in the real world where mathematical proof is applicable. Building sound arguments in practice often requires reasoning with incomplete information, uncertainty, and incomplete understanding.

The traditional approach is strongest in providing mechanisms for new patterns of thinking, for instance the idea of marginal or instantaneous change, which has many applications in describing and analyzing the world around us. But the structure of the traditional curriculum puts the mathematical thinking patterns most central to making use of information out of reach for most students.

This book seeks to provide access to the quantitative concepts and skills that can support critical thinking. A central theme is modeling, the process of building quantitative representations of real-world systems. We make use of a concrete framework for modeling that can be taught and learned. In this framework, the system components are quantities. As a concept, quantities are akin to number but involve additional information that provides basic facts about how different kinds of quantities are related to one another. There are almost no equations used. Instead, the relationships between quantities in models are represented with functions and spaces. Equally important are the ideas of rates and rates of change.

These modeling tools are also the elements of an important language to describe uncertainty and risk quantitatively. Increasingly, particularly over the last 40 years with the mainstream adoption of Bayesian inference, this language provides the means to use new observations and evidence to update our existing models.

Naturally, the point is not to build models for their own sake. They are built to allow us to extract information and conclusions from them that can inform our understanding of the real world and inform decision-making. Another important use for models is to foster communication between individuals and groups, allowing us to try to resolve through principled argument the uncertainties and disagreements that often paralyze or divide us.

Comments to instructors

The instructor interested in using this book or drawing from it to design a course is entitled to know why I have broken with many established conventions for teaching mathematics. The motivation has been a skeptical attitude: Are these conventions useful? What alternatives may be more effective for demonstrating that the mathematical topics are useful and provide new ways to think about important issues outside of mathematics?

Consider, for instance, the central role of algebra in the established curriculum. Mathematics instructors have generally been educated in the established curriculum and have come to think of algebra as an essential component of advanced mathematical thinking. We treat algebra as a way of explaining, and a framework for student evaluation. We often define student success in terms of skills in algebraic manipulation and whether the student is ready to take on a course in calculus. Some students, particularly the students we encounter in our university-level mathematics courses, have gained enough proficiency in algebraic manipulation to encourage us to use these skills as the foundation for further mathematical learning.

From the student point of view, however, the situation is different. A large fraction of students do not establish sufficient algebra skills to participate successfully in the university-level curriculum. Studies from professional organizations such as the Mathematical Association of America suggest that “a large fraction” means something like 80-90%. (To satisfy yourself that this is accurate, prompt an AI with this: “What fraction of college-bound students succeed in mastering algebra to an extent that they can use it effectively in university-level mathematics such as calculus?”)

We temper our disappointment with this lack of success with assertions like, “Calculus is hard!” and “Not everyone is cut out for mathematics.” And we make the most of our limited successes by accepting the contention that passing Calc I constitutes success.

Before accepting this state of affairs, it is worth questioning whether another approach to presenting mathematical concepts could lead to greater understanding of the quantitative concepts used throughout our economy, society, and science. My experience over 30 years of college teaching is that de-emphasizing algebraic manipulation can enable students to access not just anecdotes of mathematical analysis but the very topics that appear in the third and later semesters of the traditional calculus curriculum: functions of multiple variables, partial derivatives (and their uses), linear algebra, and differential equations.

Naturally, I am not suggesting that the students following an alternative pathway are able to do the problem sets associated with linear algebra or differential equations. But they can understand how dynamics are described and trajectories calculated. They can create an accurate mental picture of what it means to “train” a model or to evaluate trade-offs in realistic scenarios.

What are the alternatives to algebra-focused instruction? We can start by noting that most of the topics taught in a high-school level algebra course are not relevant to the skills needed for quantitative analysis of systems and data. Factoring polynomials? Solving quadratic equations? Division of polynomials? You won’t find them in this book. Better to build on foundational skills like understanding functions as a transformation of inputs to an output, and graphical interpretation of relationships described by functions.

Some, but not much, algebra is needed to understand rates of change, and an entirely adequate understanding can be developed from function graphics without formulas. Some, but not much, algebra is needed to work with dimensional analysis. The algebra of logarithms and \(e^t\) need not interfere with the development of understanding of magnitudes and the ever-increasing rate of change of exponential growth. Emphasizing functional notation rather than the idiosyncrasies of \(x^{-2}\), \(\sqrt{3}{t}\), or \(\sin^{-1}(x)\) removes many pitfalls and connects better to the computational environments (even in spreadsheets) that students will encounter in their careers.

Famously, word problems are not a strong motivator of interest in algebra. Better to set the goal as building representations of real-world systems and using these representations to extract meaningful insights and make informed decisions.

A good approximation to the convention curriculum is “teaching algebra to reach calculus.” The standard, and often encountered alternative is, “teaching algebra for its own sake.” The alternative used in this book is to teach the fundamental ideas of calculus first, with a particular emphasis on why these ideas are important and useful. One such fundamental idea is “rates of change.” This book introduces it early (chapter 4) and with only elementary algebra. The topic is used over and over again in various contexts before using the nomenclature of derivatives and accumulation: describing functions, dimensional analysis, handling trade-offs, dealing with data and hypotheses using likelihood, and modeling dynamic systems.

Overall, the style of this book is to use mathematical concepts first, and minimize or even exclude the bathwater of mathematical formalisms such as limits. The flow of topics emphasizes mathematical description rather than the precise definition of ideas in mathematical terms. The students working through the book will develop an intuitive and practical understanding of the concepts and an understanding that, with further work, they could use them in their own work and applications. And, those students who have succeeded in the conventional mathematics curriculum general find themselves with a greatly enriched understanding of the mechanisms and applied purpose of the mathematical tools they have previously learned.

  • Daniel Kaplan, October 2026, Saint Paul, Minnesota
