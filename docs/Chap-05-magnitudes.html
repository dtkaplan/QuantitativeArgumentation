5.1 Counting digits

When quantities are about the same size, it is easy to compare them to see which is bigger or smaller. For most people, the cognitive load of such comparisons is very low for values in the range 1-100. In an instant, a person can pick the larger of two quantities. Try it, which is larger: 72 or 68? No work at all.

The cognitive load rises for quantities that span a much larger range, say 1 to 10,000 or larger.

When quantities are a mixture of the very large and the very small, comparisons require attention. For instance, consider Table 5.2, which shows the gross domestic product (GDP) of a handful of countries in 2024. The table standardizes values from different countries to a standard unit: United States dollars (USD).

In Table 5.2, the numbers are “left justified”, that is, their leading digits line up in a column. Unfortunately, the leading digits—27 for Albania and 18 for China—are little help in comparing the values.

Now consider the GDP numbers, written in a different format. Table 5.3 makes it easy to see which countries have very large values and which comparatively smaller values. First, we have sorted the values. Sorting helps the eye compare every value to every other value in an instant.

The improved format of Table 5.3 also makes it clear why looking at the leading digits is not always helpful in comparing values. Instead, the first thing to look at is the number of digits. Because the values are all aligned to the right, we do not even need to count the number of digits; we can treat them as if we are looking at a bar graph. It is necessary to look to the leading digits only when the number of digits is the same.

5.2 Unit prefixes and scientific notation

As noted in the previous section, people are quick to compare or sort numbers with 1, 2, or even three digits. Bigger numbers, however, require shifting to another mode: counting digits. Similarly, comparing tiny numbers—such as 0.000032 and 0.00018—involves counting the zeros that immediately follow the decimal point.

There are two widely used techniques for formatting numbers that include information about the number of digits directly in the number. The logic of these two techniques will bring us very close to explaining how to construct graphics like Figure fig-gdp-mortality-mag.

The most familiar of the two techniques involves unit prefixes. Every day, we deal with both very large and very small distances. For instance, using base SI units, the width of a typical room is about 3 m, while a bike ride to a hardware store is about 2500 m, depending on the neighborhood. The screw bought there to fix a bike could have a diameter of 0.004 m. If these numbers sound strange, it is because everyday practice is to change the units in order to avoid counting zeros. Instead of 0.004 meters, say 4 millimeters. Rather than 2500 meters, say 2.5 kilometers.

The standard prefixes “milli” and “kilo” mean, respectively, one-thousandth and one thousand. The milli prefix—often abbreviated simply as m—moves the decimal point 3 places to the right: 0.004 m is the same as 4 mm. Likewise, kilo (abbreviated as k) moves the decimal three places to the left: 2500 m becomes 2.5 km.

The system was introduced on a limited basis by the revolutionary government of France. (See Table 5.4.) It has been extended to handle larger and smaller quantities on several occasions.

Only a handful of prefixes are in everyday use: micro, milli, kilo, mega, giga. These cover a broad range, from a tiny dose of medicine to the wealth of ultra-rich individuals. Notice that the most used prefixes each shift the decimal point by three places, enabling the range of quantities to eliminate leading or trailing zeros and be represented as numbers with one, two, or three digits plus the prefix. The 1795 prefixes that move the decimal point by 1 or 2 places have mostly fallen out of use. The most common exception is centi, which is widely used for length: centimeter (cm). The prefix hecto is in regular use to refer to area; a “hectare” is an area of (100 m)-squared, that is, (100 m)2 =  1002 m2 = 10,000 m2.

For temperatures, on the other hand, the prefix system is not used. Even without prefixes, we can write everyday temperatures with two digits.

As regards time, we have an idiomatic system for avoiding zeros: seconds, minutes, hours, days, weeks, months, years, decades, centuries, millennia. The right-moving prefixes, however, are used for time: milliseconds, microseconds, and such.

When talking about money, it is common to replace the Latin prefixes with words like “thousand,” “million,” “billion,” and “trillion.” Unfortunately, such words differ from language to language. The French use “milliard” and “billion” to mean what in English is a billion and a trillion, respectively. The Japanese use 4-digit groups for monetary quantities. “Man” is 1,0000 (that is, 10,000), “oku” is 1,0000,0000 (that is, one-hundred million), “cho” is 1,0000,0000,0000, which aligns with English “billion.”

People can easily confuse prefixes they do not use often. Such mistakes can lead to serious problems. Mistake milligrams for micrograms, and the dose of medicine will be 1,000 times too small or too large. News reports and politicians frequently confuse “million” with “billion.”

The prefix system works best when applied to units with short names, such as meters, grams, watts, joules, and so on. Things can get out of hand with derived units. For instance, a fundamental quantity used in quantum physics is Planck’s constant, which has the unusual dimension [energy] \(\times\) [time].

Planck’s constant: 0.000000000000000000000000000000000662607015 m2 kg / s

Doing calculations with such numbers is error-prone, since it is easy to omit or add zeros and hard to see at a glance that this has occurred. Writing it more compactly with prefixes is not much help:

0.663 femtometers2 gm / s

☞ Scientific notation ☜ is helpful here. To illustrate, consider a “light year,” often used to measure distances between stars. In the familiar units of kilometers, and using traditional mathematical notation, a light year is \[\color{blue}{\fbox{9.461}}\color{black}{\times 10}^{\,\color{magenta}{\fbox{12}}} .\] Such notation involves a kind of punctuation used to organize two numbers. The punctuation framework is \(\color{blue}{\fbox{ }}\color{black}{\times 10}^{\,\color{magenta}{\fbox{ }}}\). (The colors are not part of the notation. We use them to enhance clarity of what goes where.)

The two number-roles in scientific notation have standard, if obscure, names.

  • The mantissa, 9.461 in the example, is conventionally written with a single 1-to-9 digit to the left of the decimal point, 9 here. There can be any number of digits, including 0, to the right of the decimal point.
  • The exponent is placed as a superscript to 10. It is always an integer, that is, a round number. In the example, the exponent is 12.

Here are a few examples of large and small numbers in scientific notation:

  • Planck’s constant: \(\color{blue}{6.62607015} \color{black}{\times 10}^{\color{magenta}{-34}}\) m2 kg / s
  • China GDP for 2023: \(\color{blue}{1.87} \color{black}{\times 10}^{\color{magenta}{13}}\) USD
  • Value of German Mark in USD during an episode of hyperinflation: January 1923 \(\color{blue}{1.4}\color{black}{\times 10}^{\color{magenta}{-4}}\) USD; October 1923 \(\color{blue}{2.0} \color{black}{\times 10}^{\color{magenta}{-10}}\) USD;

A primary virtue of scientific notation is that the reader does not need to count digits in order to compare two numbers. The exponent takes care of the counting for you.

A flaw of this notation is that the most important number, the exponent, is written in the smallest font. In computer notation, however, the punctuation is dramatically streamlined: Planck \(\mathtt{\color{blue}{6.62607015}\color{black}{e}\!\color{magenta}{-\!34}}\); China \(\mathtt{\color{blue}{1.87}\color{black}{e}\color{magenta}{13}}\); German Mark \(\mathtt{\color{blue}{1.4}\color{black}{e}\color{magenta}{-4}}\). The e in the computer notation is shorthand for “exponent.”

Public health workers and sociologists use a variant of scientific notation with the punctuation “per.” A mortality rate of 0.0063 (deaths per person) will instead be presented as 63 deaths per 10,000 people, avoiding the need to count digits. For a rare disease, we present its prevalence across different units, e.g., cases per 100,000 people or 1,000,000 people. Naturally, 63 deaths per 10,000 people can also be sensibly written as 6.3 deaths per 1000, or, stretching things, 0.63 deaths per 100. Writing the quantity as, say, 0.063 deaths per 10 persons or 0.0063 deaths per person goes to far towards illegibility, even if mathematically justified.

The names “mantissa” and “exponent” are absolutely standard. However, the idea of “the exponent of a number” is flawed. Numbers don’t have exponents, scientific notation describes them in an exponent-based way.

However, an important idea in quantitative argumentation is “the ☞ magnitude ☜ of a number.” When comparing two numbers, the phrase ☞ order of magnitude ☜ difference can be very useful.

A good way to think about the magnitude of a number is by reference to scientific notation. The exponent in scientific notation gives a quick readout of the magnitude. For instance,

  1. 394.32 (that is, 3.9432e2) is a magnitude-2 number
  2. 0.14 (that is, 1.4e-1) is a magnitude-minus-1 number

Comparing such different sizes of numbers as (a) and (b) is often done using the magnitude. (a) and (b) are “3 orders of magnitude” different.

In sec-mag-single-num we’ll consider a more precise way to specify the magnitude of a number. The exponent in scientific notation is a rounding off of the precise magnitude.

5.3 Example: The magnitude of child mortality

We return now to the patterns in child mortality across the decades and across the world. Figure fig-gdp-mortality showed the numbers for mortality and GDP. Now we are going to look at the same data, but using magnitudes to represent the fact.

Figure fig-gdp-mortality-mag plots the mortality-vs-GDP data on axes that are arranged to highlight order-of-magnitude differences. In contrast to Figure fig-gdp-mortality the data points are much more spread out across the graphics frame. Subtle patterns are visible that involve many more countries.

For instance, there are points from 1960 that are more or less directly above many points from 2020. “Directly above” means that there is little or no change in GDP but that there is a big change in mortality. This suggests that the mortality change stems to a great extent from factors other than GDP. One such factor is the introduction in the 1970s of oral rehydration therapy, an inexpensive but effective treatment for diarrheal illnesses that were a major cause of child mortality. This style of quantitative analysis, examining the partial change in one quantity while holding another constant, is detailed in Section 4.4.

The method used to produce Figure fig-gdp-mortality-mag, the more intelligible version of the mortality-versus-GDP graph, is to set the axes’ scales to reflect the magnitude of the quantity. For ease of interpretation it is common to label the axes in a magnitude plot with corresponding numerical values rather than magnitudes. That is, the horizontal-axis labels are 1000, 10,000, and 100,000 rather than the corresponding magnitudes 3, 4, and 5. However, the magnitude value determines the spacing along the axis. Similarly, the vertical-axis labels are 0.3, 1, 3, 10, and 30. We already know that the magnitude of 1 is 0 and that the magnitude of 10 is 1. But what are the magnitudes of 0.3, 3, and 30?

5.4 Logarithms: Just the magnitude, please

Computers happily take into account the two numerals in scientific notation when doing arithmetic. But this can be hard for people. There is, however, a way to condense the two components of scientific notation into a single number conveying the same information. The basic idea is to fold the mantissa into the magnitude by allowing non-integer values for the magnitude.

The name for this condensed form of scientific notation is probably familiar to you already: ☞ logarithm ☜. Try to put aside any anxiety you associate with that name from a previous algebra course. A logarithm is just a format for writing a number.

To illustrate, consider the scientific-notation form of the 2023 GDP of China: \(\mathtt{\color{blue}{1.87}e\color{magenta}{13}}\) USD. This value is somewhat greater than \(\mathtt{\color{blue}{1}\color{black}{e}\color{magenta}{13}}\) USD but less than \(\mathtt{\color{blue}{10}\color{black}{e}\color{magenta}{13}}\) USD. Of course, \(\mathtt{\color{blue}{10}\color{black}{e}\color{magenta}{13}}\) ought to be written \(\mathtt{\color{blue}{1}\color{black}{e}\color{magenta}{14}}\). The magnitude of \(\mathtt{\color{blue}{1}\color{black}{e}\color{magenta}{13}}\) is 13, that is, the value of the exponent. Similarly, the magnitude of \(\mathtt{\color{blue}{1}\color{black}{e}\color{magenta}{14}}\) is 14.

So what is the logarithm of \(\mathtt{\color{blue}{1.87}\color{black}{e}\color{magenta}{13}}\)? Since the original number is between \(\mathtt{\color{blue}{1}\color{black}{e}\color{magenta}{13}}\) and \(\mathtt{\color{blue}{1}\color{black}{e}\color{magenta}{14}}\), its logarithm should be between 13 and 14: something like 13._ _. The digits to fill in the blanks are, to be honest, by no means obvious. Indeed, the answer was invented as recently as 1600 by John Napier (1550-1617) as an arithmetical aid. The logarithm of \(\mathtt{\color{blue}{1.87}\color{black}{e}\color{magenta}{13}}\) turns out to be 13.272, between 13 and 14, but closer to 13.

To help reinforce the connection between the magnitude in scientific notation and the logarithm, here are a few examples:

Scientific notation Logarithm
1.2325e4 4.0908
7.3e15 15.86
4.1e-8 -7.39
CautionUnits and dimensions when using logarithms

A careful consideration of units and dimensions is crucial to quantitative argumentation. Logarithms, however, only apply to pure numbers. There is no use talking about the logarithm of a kg or the dimension of a magnitude.

Comparing a quantity to another naturally requires that they two quantities have the same dimension. To use logarithms sensibly, particularly when comparing quantities, make sure to convert all the quantities to the same unit before calculating the logarithm.

Occasionally you may encounter a phrase like “log dollars.” This is not a genuine unit but a reminder that the values presented are magnitudes and that the original unit of measurement was dollars.

5.5 Order of magnitude phenomena

Many phenomena and their associated quantities are best thought of in terms of magnitude rather than number. An example: Animals, including humans, go about the world in varying states of illumination, from the bright sunlight of high noon to the dim shadows of a half-moon. To see in such diverse conditions, the eye needs to respond to light intensity over a broad range of magnitudes. When comparing two numbers of different magnitudes, the phrase used is ☞ order of magnitude. ☜

For instance, there is a difference of two orders of magnitude between 100 (1e2 in scientific notation) and 10,000 (1e4).

The lux is the unit of illuminance in the Système international. Table 5.5 shows the illumination in a range of familiar outdoor settings.

For a creature active both night and day, the eye needs to be sensitive over a range of illumination spanning more than six orders of magnitude. To accomplish this, the eyes use several mechanisms: pupil contraction or dilation accounts for about one order of magnitude; photopic (color, cones) versus scotopic (black-and-white, rods, nighttime) accounts for about three orders of magnitude; adaptation over minutes (1 order); and squinting (1 order).

More impressively, human perception of sound spans 11 orders of magnitude in the energy impinging on the eardrum. The energy density of perceptible sound ranges from the threshold of hearing at 0.000000000001 W/m2 to a conversational level of 0.000001 W/m2 to 0.1 W/m2 in the front rows of a rock concert. However, in terms of our subjective perception of loudness, each order-of-magnitude change is perceived the same way, whether it is from street traffic to a vacuum cleaner or from a whisper to everyday conversation. (The unit of sound measurement is the decibel (dB), with 10 decibels corresponding to an order of magnitude in the energy density of sound.) See Table 5.6 for the dB level of a wide range of sound levels.

6, 60, 600, and 6000 miles per hour are quantities that successively differ in size by orders of magnitude. Such differences often point to a substantial change in context. A jog is 6 mph, a car on a highway goes 60 mph, a cruising commercial jet goes 600 mph, and a rocket passes through 6000 mph on its way to orbital velocity. The range from an infant’s crawl to highway cruising covers three orders of magnitude in speed.

Of course, many phenomena are not usefully represented by orders of magnitude. For example, the difference between normal body temperature and high fever is 0.01 orders of magnitude in temperature. An increase of 1 order of magnitude in blood pressure from the normal level would cause instant death! The difference between a very tall adult and a very short adult is about 1/4 of an order of magnitude.

We are using the Kelvin scale, which is a physically meaningful scale for a ratio of temperatures.

Comparing with orders of magnitude makes sense when the relevant comparison is a ratio. “A car is 10 times faster than a person” refers to the ratio of speeds. In contrast, we generally use differences to compare quantities such as body temperature, blood pressure, and adult height. Fever is only 2\(^\circ\)C higher in temperature than normal. A 30 mmHg increase in blood pressure is worth noting as an indication of hypertension. A very tall and a very short adult differ by about 3 feet.

In medicine, blood pressure is measured using old-fashioned, non-SI units based on the height of a column of mercury that will balance blood pressure. The “mmHg” is short for “millimeters of mercury.”

Thinking in terms of orders of magnitude is appropriate when working with a set of objects whose sizes span one or many factors of 2. Comparing baseball and basketball players? No need for orders of magnitude. However, in considering the weights of mammals, from bats to whales, orders of magnitude will reveal patterns that the whales would obscure on a number-line scale.
